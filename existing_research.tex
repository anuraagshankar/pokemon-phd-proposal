\section{Existing Research}
\label{sec:existing_research}

This section surveys the work that a study of bilevel games with hard inner
equilibria must build on. It is a literature review only: it reports what has been
done and what each system demonstrates, and it leaves the question of what remains
undone to the analysis in Section~\ref{sec:analysis}. The survey is in three
parts:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item the literature on bilevel and Stackelberg games and the renowned open
        problems that share this structure, which locates the problem class;
  \item work on competitive Pok\'{e}mon, the primary application, covering both
        the shared research infrastructure and the state of the art in play; and
  \item landmark AI systems for analogous games, grouped by the strategic pillars
        they exercise.
\end{enumerate}

Per-system detail for the Pok\'{e}mon work is given in
Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix_prior_work}.

\subsection{Bilevel Games with Hard Inner Equilibria}
\label{subsec:bilevel_lit}

The problem class this study addresses, an outer decision whose payoff is the
value of an inner game, is not new in itself; it is studied as bilevel or
Stackelberg optimization, and several renowned open problems share its
structure~\cite{zhang2023bilevel}. Table~\ref{tab:bilevel} lists the anchor
references. The column that matters most is the inner problem: in the
best-understood cases it is \emph{tractable} (a unique, cheap best response),
whereas the case this study targets has a \emph{hard} inner problem, a
mixed-strategy equilibrium of an imperfect-information, stochastic, long-horizon
game. This part of the survey is a starting point and will be expanded.

\paragraph{Bilevel optimization, the framing reference.}
The survey of bilevel optimization~\cite{zhang2023bilevel} gives the formal
structure this study uses: an outer problem whose objective depends on the
solution of an inner problem, and which it explicitly connects to Stackelberg,
leader-follower, and min-max games. Crucially, it restricts its attention to
\emph{tractable} inner problems, those with a unique and reasonably cheap
solution, and notes that dropping this can make even a convex-looking bilevel
problem intractable. That restriction is exactly the boundary VGC lies beyond,
which is why the class is the right home for the problem and why VGC is not
already covered by it.

\paragraph{Stackelberg security games.}
Security games~\cite{tambe2011security} are the best-known real-world instance of
the class: a defender commits to a strategy and an attacker best-responds, with
deployed systems protecting airports and transportation networks. They establish
that the bilevel structure is a respected, consequential problem rather than a
Pok\'{e}mon artifact. Their inner problem, the attacker's best response, is
tractable, so they anchor the class's renown rather than its difficulty.

\paragraph{Adversarial robustness.}
Robust training~\cite{madry2018towards} poses a saddle-point problem: the outer
level minimizes over model parameters while the inner level maximizes adversarial
loss. It is a hard-inner sibling, since the inner maximization is non-convex, and
it links adversarial machine learning to the bilevel framing because min-max
optimization is a special case of bilevel optimization.

\paragraph{Unsupervised environment design.}
Environment design~\cite{dennis2020emergent} makes the outer decision the choice
of a training environment and the inner problem a reinforcement-learning agent's
learned behavior in it, with an adversary shaping a curriculum through regret.
Its inner problem is a full RL equilibrium, which makes it the closest sibling in
spirit to VGC, where the inner game must likewise be learned rather than solved in
closed form.

\subsection{Competitive Pok\'{e}mon (VGC)}
\label{subsec:vgc_application}

Competitive Pok\'{e}mon is the primary application of this study, and it is the
sharpest concrete instance of a bilevel game with a hard inner equilibrium. This
subsection covers the shared research infrastructure and then the state of the art
in play. One distinction runs throughout: competitive Pok\'{e}mon is played in two
formats, singles and doubles, and the two are studied very unevenly, so the format
of each system is recorded explicitly because it most shapes how the prior work
bears on a VGC study.

\subsubsection{Research Infrastructure}
\label{subsec:infrastructure}

Nearly all modern Pok\'{e}mon AI research runs on a shared stack: the Pok\'{e}mon
Showdown simulator, the poke-env Python interface to it, and a small number of
public benchmarks built on top. Table~\ref{tab:infrastructure} lists these. They
provide executable mechanics, machine-readable logs, and standardized evaluation,
but were built mainly around singles play, with doubles and VGC support added
later and less completely.

\subsection{Analogous Games and Their AI Systems}
\label{subsec:analogous}

Having located the problem class and its primary application, we now zoom out to
the games whose AI systems a VGC study would draw on, grouped by which of VGC's
strategic pillars each exercises. VGC's difficulty comes from five strategic
pillars, as set out in Section~\ref{sec:introduction}: a structural first pillar
and four that make the inner battling game hard.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item the bilevel structure (team building wrapping battling);
  \item long-horizon planning;
  \item simultaneous action;
  \item imperfect information; and
  \item stochasticity.
\end{enumerate}

No single solved game combines all five, but several landmark systems each solved
a game that shares one or more of the inner pillars (2--5), and these are the
systems whose methods a VGC study would draw on. They are grouped below by
strategic theme; each group's heading and caption list the pillars its systems
exercise, by the numbers above, and each table gives one row per system. The
notation $3^{*}$ marks a weaker form of pillar~3: real-time concurrency, where
both players act at once but without the blind simultaneous commitment (and
resulting matrix-game mixed strategies) that true pillar~3 requires. Notably,
none of the reviewed single-game systems exercises pillar~1: each solves a game
with no outer team-building level, which is the gap developed in
Section~\ref{sec:analysis}. They are reviewed here as methodological precedent;
the argument that none of them covers VGC's full combination is developed there.
