\section{Existing Research}
\label{sec:existing_research}

This section surveys the work that a study of competitive Pok\'{e}mon AI must
build on. It is a literature review only: it reports what has been done and what
each system demonstrates, and it leaves the question of what remains undone to
the analysis in Section~\ref{sec:analysis}. The survey is in four parts:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item the shared infrastructure on which recent work depends;
  \item the state of the art in competitive Pok\'{e}mon \emph{singles};
  \item the smaller body of work that targets the VGC \emph{doubles} format
        directly; and
  \item landmark AI systems for analogous games.
\end{enumerate}

The first three parts are presented as summary tables below; per-system detail is
given in Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix_prior_work}.

One distinction runs through the whole survey and is worth stating at the outset.
Competitive Pok\'{e}mon is played in two formats, singles and doubles, and the
two are studied very unevenly. The format of each system is therefore recorded
explicitly, because it is the fact that most shapes how the prior work bears on a
VGC study.

\subsection{Research Infrastructure}
\label{subsec:infrastructure}

Nearly all modern Pok\'{e}mon AI research runs on a shared stack: the Pok\'{e}mon
Showdown simulator, the poke-env Python interface to it, and a small number of
public benchmarks built on top. Table~\ref{tab:infrastructure} lists these. They
provide executable mechanics, machine-readable logs, and standardized evaluation,
but were built mainly around singles play, with doubles and VGC support added
later and less completely.

\subsection{Competitive Pok\'{e}mon Singles: State of the Art}
\label{subsec:singles}

The strongest published battle results in competitive Pok\'{e}mon are in the
singles format. Table~\ref{tab:singles} lists the systems that define the current
state of the art. Together they map the two methodological families a VGC study
is most likely to inherit: large-scale learning from human data, and search
guided by learned or language-model priors. Every entry targets singles.

\small

                                >{\hsize=1.50\hsize\raggedright\arraybackslash}X@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{System} & \textbf{Method} & \textbf{Format} & \textbf{Key result} \\
\midrule

  & Offline RL + imitation; Transformer policies up to 200M parameters, trained
    from human replays reconstructed into first-person trajectories
  & Singles (Gen 1--4)
  & Best agents climb into the top 10\% of active players on the Showdown ladder.
    Closest method analog to a deep-learning VGC approach. \\
\addlinespace

  & Minimax search with LLM-based action proposal, opponent modeling, and value

  & Singles (Gen 9 OU)
  & Strong win rates against prior LLM and rule-based agents; compiles a large
    human-game dataset. Evidence for a search + learned-prior hybrid. \\

    Foundational for the LLM line; modest strength. \\
\addlinespace
Earlier self-play / search agents \cite{lee2017showdown}
  & Self-play RL and search on Showdown
  & Singles
  & Pre-LLM baselines; established the target but did not reach the strength

\label{subsec:vgc}

dedicated, and thinner than the singles literature, but it includes the most
important reference point for this study (VGC-Bench). Table~\ref{tab:vgc} records
each system together with whether it runs on the real game engine or an abstracted
model, the distinction that governs how directly its results transfer.

\subsection{Analogous Games and Their AI Systems}
\label{subsec:analogous}

VGC's difficulty comes from combining four strategic properties at once, as set
out in Section~\ref{sec:introduction}: (1) long-horizon planning (2) simultaneous action (3) imperfect information (4) stochasticity.

No single solved game combines all four, but several landmark systems each solved
a game that shares one or more of them, and these are the systems whose methods a
VGC study would draw on. They are grouped below by strategic theme; each group's
heading and caption list the pillars its systems exercise, by the numbers above,
and each table gives one row per system. The notation $2^{*}$ marks a weaker form
of pillar~2: real-time concurrency, where both players act at once but without the
blind simultaneous commitment (and resulting matrix-game mixed strategies) that
true pillar~2 requires. They are reviewed here as methodological precedent; the
argument that none of them covers VGC's full combination is developed in
Section~\ref{sec:analysis}.
