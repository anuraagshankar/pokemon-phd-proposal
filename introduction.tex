\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

To most people, Pok\'{e}mon is the single-player adventure of catching creatures
and working through a story campaign. Running alongside it, however, is a whole
parallel track of \emph{competitive} Pok\'{e}mon, where two human players battle
each other under tournament rules. It is this competitive track, not the
single-player game, that this study is concerned with.

\subsection{Competitive Pok\'{e}mon and the VGC Format}

Competitive Pok\'{e}mon is a far harder game than the single-player campaign
suggests. It is played in two main formats:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Singles:} one active Pok\'{e}mon per side; the basis of the
        community-run ladders.
  \item \textbf{Doubles:} two of a player's Pok\'{e}mon on the field at once,
        each able to act on, support, or target either opponent. This is the
        format of the official \emph{Pok\'{e}mon Video Game Championships} (VGC),
        run by The Pok\'{e}mon Company and contested at Regional, International,
        and World Championship level.
\end{itemize}

VGC defines official, top-level human play, so it is the only setting in which
``superhuman'' performance is a well-posed target. For that reason it is the
sole focus of this study; other formats appear only for comparison. Its rules
make VGC a partial-information game with simultaneous moves, not the
full-information, take-turns game its surface presentation suggests~\cite{traylor_vgc}:

\begin{itemize}
  \item A team has six Pok\'{e}mon, but only four are brought into any battle.
  \item The opponent's four-from-six selection is hidden until revealed in play;
        the two benched Pok\'{e}mon are never seen.
  \item Both players choose moves at the same time each turn, with no alternating
        turn order.
  \item A tournament round is a best-of-three set: the same two players play up to
        three battles, and the winner is the first to take two. Players adapt their
        four-from-six selection and plans across games within the set.
  \item At official tournaments, \emph{Open Team Sheets} disclose full movesets,
        items, abilities, and Terastal types before the match, but lead selection
        and in-battle plans stay hidden.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{A Unified Competitive Platform: Pok\'{e}mon Champions}

\begin{itemize}
  \item Historically, VGC was hosted on whichever mainline role-playing title was
        current, so competitive tools were built on top of games not designed for
        them, and the metagame moved generation to generation.
  \item \emph{Pok\'{e}mon Champions} (developed by The Pok\'{e}mon Works;
        published by The Pok\'{e}mon Company and Nintendo) is a dedicated
        competitive battle simulator, released on Nintendo Switch in April 2026
        and on iOS and Android in June 2026~\cite{pokemon_champions_wiki}.
  \item It is set to replace the mainline titles as the official VGC tournament
        platform~\cite{pokemon_champions_official}, giving competitive Pok\'{e}mon
        a single purpose-built platform and a more stable base than the
        rotating-cartridge approach it replaces.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{The Two Phases of Competitive Pok\'{e}mon}

Competitive Pok\'{e}mon is a repeating, two-stage game~\cite{traylor_vgc}:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Team building} (planning): select and fully customize a roster of
        six Pok\'{e}mon from a pool of over a thousand species and hundreds of
        moves, items, and abilities.
  \item \textbf{Battling} (execution): put those choices into play against an
        opponent.
\end{itemize}

The two cannot be cleanly separated: a good team requires knowing how battles are
won, and winning battles requires a well-built team. The two phases nest. Battling
is the inner problem of playing a fixed pair of teams well, and team building is
the outer problem of choosing a team whose value is set by how it fares in those
battles. This study treats the full coupled problem as its framing, but commits
firmly to the inner phase and reaches into team building as far as is feasible,
rather than promising to solve the outer problem in full. Section~\ref{sec:analysis}
develops this nesting and the bound on how much of it one project can claim.

\subsection{The Four Strategic Pillars of Battling}

Following Traylor~\cite{traylor_vgc}, high-level VGC battling demands four parts
of game theory at once:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Long-term planning.} As in chess, resources shrink as Pok\'{e}mon
        are knocked out. Early actions shape later board states, so the
        highest-damage move now is often worse than one that improves position.
        A zero-damage move, such as sacrificing a Pok\'{e}mon to set up a
        speed-doubling effect, can decide the game several turns later.
  \item \textbf{Simultaneous action selection.} Both players choose moves without
        seeing the opponent's choice, then resolve together, adding a
        rock--paper--scissors guessing element. Mechanics like Terastallization
        (an on-command type change, once per battle) turn these blind choices into
        a branching set of outcomes, not a single flat decision.
  \item \textbf{Imperfect information.}\footnote{We list imperfect information and
        simultaneous action selection as separate pillars for exposition, but they
        are not mutually exclusive. Simultaneous action selection is itself a form
        of imperfect information: when both players commit at the same time, each
        acts without knowing the other's current choice, which is exactly a hidden
        piece of information. Formally, simultaneous-move games reduce to
        imperfect-information games~\cite{sokota2023abstractingimperfectinformationaway}. The two pillars
        therefore overlap; we keep them distinct only to separate the information
        hidden \emph{across} turns (bench, movesets) from the information hidden
        \emph{within} a turn (the opponent's simultaneous choice).} A player acts
        knowing two unrevealed Pok\'{e}mon wait on the opponent's bench, and that
        revealed Pok\'{e}mon may carry unexpected moves or items. Open Team Sheets
        help but do not remove this: lead selection, benched threats, and in-battle
        plans stay hidden.
  \item \textbf{Probability management.} Move accuracy, secondary effects (e.g.
        status), critical hits, and damage rolls all inject chance. Strong play
        is not avoiding chance but managing it, choosing lines that hold up across
        the range of possible results.
\end{itemize}

What sets VGC apart is that all four pillars are present \emph{at the same time}.
Benchmark games isolate only some: chess demands long-term planning but under full
information and turn order; poker combines imperfect information with chance but is
turn-based. VGC sits at the meeting point of all four~\cite{traylor_vgc}, and, as
this document argues, no solved benchmark game shares that combination.

\subsection{Thesis and Research Questions}

This document is a feasibility study asking whether superhuman performance in
Pok\'{e}mon VGC is an achievable and worthwhile target for doctoral research,
across both the battling and team-building phases described above and the way they
nest. It does not yet commit to a method; it describes the problem, surveys what
has been tried, and assesses where the open frontier lies. It is organized around
three core questions:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Complexity.} How computationally and strategically complex is
        Pok\'{e}mon VGC as a problem domain, and how does that complexity compare
        with that of strategy games already studied in AI?
  \item \textbf{Existing work.} What prior research exists on AI agents for
        competitive Pok\'{e}mon, and on similar imperfect-information,
        simultaneous-action games such as poker, Stratego, and StarCraft~II?
  \item \textbf{Gap analysis.} If superhuman VGC performance has not been
        achieved, why not, and what would it take to close that gap?
\end{enumerate}

The rest of this document is structured as follows.
Section~\ref{sec:existing_research} reviews the existing research on competitive
Pok\'{e}mon AI and on similar game-AI systems.
Section~\ref{sec:analysis} analyzes the complexity of the domain, draws out the
gap that the prior work leaves open, and assesses how feasible it is to close it.
