\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

\subsection{Bilevel Games with Hard Inner Equilibria}

Many important decisions have a two-level shape. A decision maker first commits
to a choice, and the value of that choice is then decided by a second problem
that must be played out or solved underneath it. Choose a defensive allocation,
and its value is set by how a strategic attacker responds. Choose a training
environment, and its value is set by how well an agent learns to act in it.
Choose the parameters of a model, and their value is set by a worst-case
perturbation. Problems of this kind, where an outer decision's payoff is the
value of an inner optimization or game, are studied as \emph{bilevel} (or
\emph{Stackelberg}) problems~\cite{zhang2023bilevel}. They appear across
well-known areas of research: Stackelberg security games, deployed to protect
airports and other infrastructure~\cite{tambe2011security}; adversarial
robustness, posed as a min-max saddle-point problem~\cite{madry2018towards}; and
unsupervised environment design, where an adversary shapes a curriculum for a
learning agent~\cite{dennis2020emergent}.

In the cases that are best understood, the inner problem is
\emph{tractable}: its solution is unique and reasonably cheap to compute, so the
outer problem can be attacked with the machinery built for that setting. The
survey that frames this class restricts attention to exactly such tractable
cases, and notes that even a bilevel problem with convex levels can be NP-hard
once that structure is lost~\cite{zhang2023bilevel}. This leaves a natural
question open. What happens when the inner problem is not tractable at all, but a
genuinely hard game: one with imperfect information, simultaneous moves, chance,
and a long horizon, whose solution is a mixed-strategy equilibrium rather than a
single cheap answer? We refer to this underserved case as a \emph{bilevel game
with a hard inner equilibrium}, and it is the subject of this study.

Competitive Pok\'{e}mon, in its official doubles format, is the sharpest concrete
instance of this case that we are aware of, and it is the primary application
through which we develop and test the ideas here. The outer decision is building
a team; the inner problem is winning battles with it; and a team's value is the
value of the battling equilibrium it induces. The rest of this section builds up
that instance and then states the thesis question in these terms.

\subsection{Motivation: Competitive Pok\'{e}mon and VGC}

To most people, Pok\'{e}mon is the single-player adventure of catching creatures
and working through a story campaign. Running alongside it, however, is a whole
parallel track of \emph{competitive} Pok\'{e}mon, where two human players battle
each other under tournament rules. It is this competitive track, not the
single-player game, that this study draws on.

Competitive Pok\'{e}mon is a far harder game than the single-player campaign
suggests. It is played in two main formats:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Singles:} one active Pok\'{e}mon per side; the basis of the
        community-run ladders.
  \item \textbf{Doubles:} two of a player's Pok\'{e}mon on the field at once,
        each able to act on, support, or target either opponent. This is the
        format of the official \emph{Pok\'{e}mon Video Game Championships} (VGC),
        run by The Pok\'{e}mon Company and contested at Regional, International,
        and World Championship level.
\end{itemize}

VGC defines official, top-level human play, so it is the only setting in which
``superhuman'' performance is a well-posed target. For that reason it is the
format we use; other formats appear only for comparison. Its rules make VGC a
partial-information game with simultaneous moves, not the full-information,
take-turns game its surface presentation suggests~\cite{traylor_vgc}:

\begin{itemize}
  \item A team has six Pok\'{e}mon, but only four are brought into any battle.
  \item The opponent's four-from-six selection is hidden until revealed in play;
        the two benched Pok\'{e}mon are never seen.
  \item Both players choose moves at the same time each turn, with no alternating
        turn order.
  \item A tournament round is a best-of-three set: the same two players play up to
        three battles, and the winner is the first to take two. Players adapt their
        four-from-six selection and plans across games within the set.
  \item At official tournaments, \emph{Open Team Sheets} disclose full movesets,
        items, abilities, and Terastal types before the match, but lead selection
        and in-battle plans stay hidden.
\end{itemize}

Historically, VGC was hosted on whichever mainline role-playing title was
current, so competitive tools were built on top of games not designed for them,
and the metagame moved generation to generation. \emph{Pok\'{e}mon Champions}
(developed by The Pok\'{e}mon Works; published by The Pok\'{e}mon Company and
Nintendo) is a dedicated competitive battle simulator, released on Nintendo
Switch in April 2026 and on iOS and Android in June
2026~\cite{pokemon_champions_wiki}. It is set to replace the mainline titles as
the official VGC tournament platform~\cite{pokemon_champions_official}, giving
competitive Pok\'{e}mon a single purpose-built platform and a more stable base
than the rotating-cartridge approach it replaces.

\subsection{VGC as a Bilevel Game with a Hard Inner Game}

Competitive Pok\'{e}mon is a repeating, two-stage game~\cite{traylor_vgc}:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Team building} (planning): select and fully customize a roster of
        six Pok\'{e}mon from a pool of over a thousand species and hundreds of
        moves, items, and abilities.
  \item \textbf{Battling} (execution): put those choices into play against an
        opponent.
\end{itemize}

These two stages are exactly the outer and inner levels of the abstraction in
Section~\ref{sec:introduction}. Team building is the \emph{outer} decision, and
battling is the \emph{inner} game whose equilibrium sets a team's value. The two
cannot be cleanly separated: a good team requires knowing how battles are won,
and winning battles requires a well-built team. The two phases nest. Battling is
the inner problem of playing a fixed pair of teams well, and team building is the
outer problem of choosing a team whose value is set by how it fares in those
battles. What makes VGC the hard-inner instance, rather than one of the tractable
cases, is that the inner game is not a cheap unique best response but an
equilibrium of an imperfect-information, simultaneous-move, stochastic,
long-horizon game. The next subsection sets out why that inner game is hard.

This study treats the full coupled problem as its framing, but commits firmly to
the inner phase and reaches into team building as far as is feasible, rather than
promising to solve the outer problem in full. Section~\ref{sec:analysis} develops
this nesting and the bound on how much of it one project can claim.

\subsection{The Four Strategic Pillars: Why the Inner Game Is Hard}

The inner game is hard because high-level VGC battling demands four parts of game
theory at once~\cite{traylor_vgc}:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Long-term planning.} As in chess, resources shrink as Pok\'{e}mon
        are knocked out. Early actions shape later board states, so the
        highest-damage move now is often worse than one that improves position.
        A zero-damage move, such as sacrificing a Pok\'{e}mon to set up a
        speed-doubling effect, can decide the game several turns later.
  \item \textbf{Simultaneous action selection.} Both players choose moves without
        seeing the opponent's choice, then resolve together, adding a
        rock--paper--scissors guessing element. Mechanics like Terastallization
        (an on-command type change, once per battle) turn these blind choices into
        a branching set of outcomes, not a single flat decision.
  \item \textbf{Imperfect information.}\footnote{We list imperfect information and
        simultaneous action selection as separate pillars for exposition, but they
        are not mutually exclusive. Simultaneous action selection is itself a form
        of imperfect information: when both players commit at the same time, each
        acts without knowing the other's current choice, which is exactly a hidden
        piece of information. Formally, simultaneous-move games reduce to
        imperfect-information games~\cite{sokota2023abstractingimperfectinformationaway}. The two pillars
        therefore overlap; we keep them distinct only to separate the information
        hidden \emph{across} turns (bench, movesets) from the information hidden
        \emph{within} a turn (the opponent's simultaneous choice).} A player acts
        knowing two unrevealed Pok\'{e}mon wait on the opponent's bench, and that
        revealed Pok\'{e}mon may carry unexpected moves or items. Open Team Sheets
        help but do not remove this: lead selection, benched threats, and in-battle
        plans stay hidden.
  \item \textbf{Probability management.} Move accuracy, secondary effects (e.g.
        status), critical hits, and damage rolls all inject chance. Strong play
        is not avoiding chance but managing it, choosing lines that hold up across
        the range of possible results.
\end{itemize}

What sets VGC apart is that all four pillars are present \emph{at the same time}.
Benchmark games isolate only some: chess demands long-term planning but under full
information and turn order; poker combines imperfect information with chance but is
turn-based. VGC sits at the meeting point of all four~\cite{traylor_vgc}, and, as
this document argues, no solved benchmark game shares that combination. This is
what makes the inner equilibrium hard, and what places VGC outside the tractable
bilevel cases described in Section~\ref{sec:introduction}.

\subsection{Thesis and Research Questions}

This document is a feasibility study asking whether \emph{bilevel games with hard
inner equilibria} are an achievable and worthwhile target for doctoral research,
using competitive Pok\'{e}mon VGC as the primary application. It does not yet
commit to a method; it describes the problem, surveys what has been tried, and
assesses where the open frontier lies. It is organized around three core
questions:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Problem class and complexity.} What characterizes a bilevel game
        with a hard inner equilibrium, how does it differ from the tractable
        bilevel and Stackelberg settings already studied, and how complex is VGC
        as an instance of it?
  \item \textbf{Existing work.} What prior research exists on bilevel and
        Stackelberg games, on equilibrium-finding in imperfect-information
        stochastic games such as poker and Stratego, on the renowned open-problem
        siblings of this class, and on AI agents for competitive Pok\'{e}mon in
        particular?
  \item \textbf{Gap analysis.} If this class of problem is not solved, why not,
        what would a first credible attack look like, and where does VGC sit in
        that gap?
\end{enumerate}

The rest of this document is structured as follows.
Section~\ref{sec:existing_research} reviews the existing research on this problem
class and on similar game-AI systems, including competitive Pok\'{e}mon AI.
Section~\ref{sec:analysis} analyzes the complexity of the domain, draws out the
gap that the prior work leaves open, and assesses how feasible it is to close it.
