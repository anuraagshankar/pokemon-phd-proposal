\subsection{Formalizing VGC as a Game}
\label{subsec:formalization}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
% DONE (H) State the formal model explicitly: VGC battling is a partially observable
%     stochastic game (POSG) with simultaneous moves, not an MDP.
%     [src: claude_deep_research §8.2; chatgpt_deep_research Stage 1]
\paragraph{The formal model.}
We model a VGC battle as a \emph{partially observable stochastic game} (POSG):
a game between two strategic players, in which each player observes only part of
the underlying game state, and in which the outcome of a turn is
stochastic~\cite{karten2025pokeagent,perolat2022deepnash}. Partial observability
comes from the hidden information VGC always carries: the opponent's two benched
Pok\'{e}mon, their four-from-six lead selection until it is revealed, and, even
under Open Team Sheets, their exact stat allocation. Stochasticity comes from the
battle mechanics themselves, including damage rolls, move accuracy, critical hits,
and secondary effects. This places VGC in the same family as the
imperfect-information games studied in the poker and Stratego literature, whose
solution concepts we return to in Section~\ref{subsec:positioning}.
% DONE (H) Spell out the tuple: state = full hidden simulator state; observation =
%     what the player legally knows (own team, revealed opponent info, OTS);
%     action = joint choice for both active Pokemon; transition = stochastic
%     simulator mechanics; reward = win/loss (+ optional shaping).
%     [src: chatgpt_deep_research Stage 1]
\paragraph{The game tuple.}
The model is given by the following components~\cite{karten2025pokeagent}:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{State:} the full hidden simulator state, that is, everything the
        battle engine tracks: both complete teams, their exact stats, current HP,
        field conditions, and what each side has already used.
  \item \textbf{Observation:} what one player legally knows, namely their own team
        in full, all revealed opponent information, and the Open Team Sheet
        disclosure (movesets, items, abilities, and Terastal types). It excludes
        the opponent's hidden lead selection, their two benched Pok\'{e}mon, and
        their exact stat allocation.
  \item \textbf{Action:} a joint choice for both of a side's active Pok\'{e}mon,
        each one selecting a move or a switch, together with a target and the
        once-per-battle option to Terastallize. This joint structure is the main
        reason the per-turn action space is large, as quantified in
        Section~\ref{subsec:complexity}.
  \item \textbf{Transition:} the simulator's stochastic mechanics, which resolve
        both players' joint actions into the next state.
  \item \textbf{Reward:} the terminal win or loss of the battle, a sparse signal
        that may optionally be shaped to aid training.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{One controller or two cooperating agents?}
The tuple above treats each side as a single controller that issues one joint
action, which is the simplest faithful model. A side's action, however,
decomposes into the choices of its two active Pok\'{e}mon, so a side may instead
be modeled as two cooperating agents that share the same win-or-loss reward and
must coordinate their moves, for example one Pok\'{e}mon setting up a
speed-doubling effect or redirecting attacks while the other attacks. Under this
view VGC is a mixed game: cooperation within a side and competition between the
two sides. Whether to treat a side as one controller or as two cooperating agents
is therefore a modeling decision with consequences for the method, and one we
return to in the analysis that follows.

%     - ACROSS a best-of-three set and across matches: opponents mind-read from
%       prior play -> this is the non-stationarity that breaks the single-shot
%       Markov view. Handle via I-POMDP / opponent-modeling framing, or by

\paragraph{The Markov question within one battle.}
The full simulator state satisfies the Markov property: given the complete engine
state, the distribution over the next state does not depend on how that state was
reached. The player's \emph{observation} does not satisfy it, because hidden
information means a player's belief about the true state depends on the whole
history of what has been revealed. This is the precise sense in which a battle is
partially observable, a POMDP from the player's seat, even though the state
underneath is Markov. A second, more subtle caveat is worth flagging. Even setting
hidden information aside, a near-identical state can recur within a single battle,
with everything equal but the remaining Power Points, and an opponent reading
prior play may choose a different action the second time. The player's decision
problem \emph{may} therefore be history-dependent, in which case a Markov state for
that problem would have to include the interaction history rather than the board
alone. We stress the word may: it remains possible that an agent learns optimal
play without conditioning on past moves. We raise this as an open caveat, not a
settled requirement.

\paragraph{The Markov question across a set and across matches.}
The same possible history-dependence reappears over a longer horizon. In a
best-of-three set, and across a tournament, opponents adapt to prior play, so the
opponent may behave as a non-stationary part of the environment and the
single-shot Markov view over one battle may not capture it. If this dependence
turns out to matter, two natural responses are to lift the unit of analysis to the
whole best-of-three set, folding the cross-game history into the state, or to carry
a belief over the opponent's type and model them explicitly. As above, this is a
contingency to plan for rather than a fact we assert.
% DONE (H) Note the simultaneous-move consequence: each turn is a matrix game ->
%     solution concept is a (possibly mixed) equilibrium, not a max-reward action.
%     Forward-reference subsec:lineage. [src: vgc_comparison Q2]
\paragraph{The simultaneous-move consequence.}
Because both players commit their actions at the same time each turn, a single
turn is not a sequential decision but a simultaneous-move, normal-form game: a
matrix game between the two sides' available actions. The right answer to such a
turn is therefore a possibly mixed, that is randomized, equilibrium rather than a
single highest-reward action, since any deterministic best response is exploitable
in a guessing game of this kind~\cite{lanctot2014smmcts}. What machinery produces
such equilibria is taken up in Section~\ref{subsec:positioning}. The same
equilibrium logic recurs one level up, at the team-building layer: there is no
single best team, because for any team a counter-team can be built, so the
team-level solution is itself an equilibrium, a team (or distribution over teams)
that balances against the field rather than one that beats everything. We develop
this coupling between team building and battling in
Section~\ref{subsec:triviality}.

\subsection{Quantifying the Complexity}
\label{subsec:complexity}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
% DONE [TODO 3] (I) Give the numbers, with assumptions stated, not rhetoric:
%     - team-config space: ~10^139 (Gen 9, VGC-Bench) AND ~6x10^218 (USUM,
%       Traylor) -> present BOTH, state era + granularity, do NOT silently pick.
%     - per-turn stochastic branching factor: up to ~10^12.
%     - OTS information-set size: lower-bounded ~10^58.
%     [src: claude_deep_research §0, §1.1, §5; vgc_aaron_traylor §5]
\paragraph{The raw numbers.}
Three published figures quantify the size of the problem. Each is stated below
with the era and the degrees of freedom it counts, since these assumptions matter.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Team-configuration space.} Estimates of the number of legal teams
        range from about $10^{139}$ (Generation 9, counting species, moves, items,
        abilities, the 19 Terastal types, and stat allocations) to about
        $6.087 \times 10^{218}$ (the Ultra Sun and Ultra Moon
        era)~\cite{angliss2026vgcbench,traylor_vgc}. The two differ by generation
        and by which degrees of freedom are counted, so we present both rather
        than silently choosing one. Both exceed the configuration space of every
        other benchmarked strategy game by many orders of magnitude.
  \item \textbf{Per-turn stochastic branching factor.} The number of distinct
        outcomes a single turn can resolve into, driven by the stochastic
        mechanics, reaches as high as about $10^{12}$~\cite{angliss2026vgcbench}.
  \item \textbf{Information-set size.} Even under Open Team Sheets, the size of a
        player's information set is lower-bounded at about $10^{58}$, because the
        opponent's exact stat allocation remains hidden~\cite{angliss2026vgcbench}.
\end{itemize}
% DONE [TODO 1] (singles vs doubles) Fold in here as WHY the numbers are large:
%     two active Pokemon -> joint action space, targeting, Protect/positioning,
%     speed control, coordination; bring-6-choose-4 + hidden lead. Doubles is not
%     "singles x2". [src: claude_deep_research §3.1, §6.2; introduction.tex]
\paragraph{Why doubles, not singles.}
The size of these numbers comes from the doubles structure, not from scaling
singles up. With two active Pok\'{e}mon per side, each turn is a joint choice over
both, with targeting, Protect and positioning, speed control, and coordination
between the two, on top of the bring-six-choose-four selection and hidden lead.
These factors combine multiplicatively, so doubles is structurally harder than two
singles games at once rather than simply twice as large.
% DONE (J) CREDIBILITY GUARDRAIL: say WHICH definition of complexity is meant.
%     Under raw state-space / game-tree size VGC is SMALL next to Go/SC2 -> the
%     defensible claim is game-theoretic DEPTH under variance, not scale.
%     Do not overclaim to the advisor. [src: vgc_comparison Q2]
\paragraph{Which definition of complexity.}
It matters which notion of complexity these numbers support. Under raw
state-space or game-tree size, a VGC battle is in fact \emph{small} next to Go or
StarCraft~II: it is a handful of Pok\'{e}mon over tens of turns, and a single
battle with fixed teams can be self-played millions of times on modest hardware.
The defensible claim to make is therefore not one of scale but of game-theoretic
depth under variance: VGC stacks simultaneous commitment, imperfect information,
and genuine randomness together, and it is that combination, not the size of the
tree, that makes it hard. A second source of difficulty that scale alone misses is
that the two optimization problems are nested: team building is an outer problem
that wraps the inner battling problem, so the difficulty compounds across layers
rather than residing in any single game tree. We develop this nesting in
Section~\ref{subsec:triviality}. We are careful not to overclaim: the assertion
that VGC is more complex than StarCraft~II is false under the computational-scale
reading, and we do not make it.

\subsection{Where VGC Sits Among Solved Games}
\label{subsec:positioning}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
% DONE [TODO 2] (difference from poker / StarCraft / etc.) Build on the four-pillars
%     table in existing_research; here make the ANALYTICAL point, not the survey:
%     no solved benchmark combines all four axes at once; VGC's hard axis
%     (both players guess at the same time + real randomness) is exactly the axis
%     AlphaStar is WEAKEST on. [src: vgc_comparison Q2; existing_research tables]
\paragraph{The axis no benchmark covers.}
The survey in Section~\ref{sec:existing_research} shows that no solved benchmark
exercises all four strategic pillars at once. VGC's hard axis is pillar~2 in its
true form, both players committing blind at the same time, combined with pillar~4,
genuine randomness. That is the corner of the design space no prior system has
had to solve. AlphaStar is weakest precisely here: its concurrency is the
real-time form marked $2^{*}$ in Section~\ref{sec:existing_research}, not blind
simultaneous commitment, and StarCraft~II carries essentially no pillar-4
randomness. The most cited ``we beat a hard game'' system is therefore the one
least equipped for the axis on which VGC is hard~\cite{vinyals2019alphastar}.

%     - In a guessing game, an agent that just tries to win the most chases its
%       own tail (spam Rock -> opponent spams Paper -> switch to Scissors ->
%       ...). It never settles. VGC-Bench saw exactly this (rock-paper-scissors
%       cycles among strong agents). [src: vgc_comparison Q2; claude_deep_research §1.1]
%     - The fix is still deep RL, but wrapped so it aims for an UNEXPLOITABLE

\paragraph{Why plain deep RL is not enough.}
In a guessing game, an agent that simply maximizes reward chases its own tail: it
spams one move, the opponent learns to punish it, it switches, and so on, never
settling on a stable strategy. This is not hypothetical for VGC. VGC-Bench observed
exactly such rock-paper-scissors cycles among its strongest
agents~\cite{angliss2026vgcbench}. The fix is still deep RL, but aimed at an
\emph{unexploitable} strategy rather than the highest-reward one. That is the
equilibrium machinery: regularized Nash dynamics as in
DeepNash~\cite{perolat2022deepnash}, fictitious self-play as in
NFSP~\cite{heinrich2016nfsp}, run inside population training such as
PSRO~\cite{lanctot2017psro}. This fixes the lineage cleanly. AlphaStar's
population and league idea transfers directly, because it is precisely its tool
for non-transitivity, which VGC shares. What does not transfer is its core: each
agent still maximizes reward and converges to a deterministic best response, which
is fine in StarCraft~II but exploitable in VGC, where every turn is a blind
simultaneous-commit matrix game whose unexploitable solution is a mixed strategy.
That per-decision equilibrium is what the poker and Stratego line adds. Deep RL
remains the engine, and equilibrium-seeking is what we wrap around it.

\subsection{The Triviality Risk and What Dissolves It}
\label{subsec:triviality}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------

%      Also confirm the exact "1 -> 30" figures. Resource summary says so; the

\paragraph{The triviality risk, and why it does not hold.}
The serious worry is that the project reduces to engineering: take an existing
method such as regularized Nash dynamics or counterfactual regret minimization,
wire it to a Pok\'{e}mon environment, add compute, and report a win rate. That
would be a strong engineering project, not a PhD. The newest evidence both grants
the worry and dissolves it. A deep RL agent already defeats a professional VGC
competitor, but only in a single-team mirror match, the same one team on both
sides, so it never faces team diversity at all; performance then collapses as the
number of training teams is scaled from one up to thirty~\cite{angliss2026vgcbench}.
Winning one fixed mirror matchup is therefore close to solved, and that narrow
result cannot be the headline. The open problem is play that generalizes across
teams and that opponents cannot exploit.
% DONE (B) Why exploitability + non-transitivity are the real targets, plainly:
%     even the strongest agents can be beaten by an opponent that studies them
%     (exploitable), and strong agents beat each other in circles (A>B>C>A). So
%     the goal is a strategy nobody can punish (an unexploitable equilibrium), not
%     just one that scores high. This is what the equilibrium machinery buys us.
%     [src: claude_deep_research §1.1]
\paragraph{Exploitability and non-transitivity are the real targets.}
Two facts about even the strongest VGC agents define what success has to mean.
They remain exploitable: an opponent that studies them can reliably learn to beat
them. They are also non-transitive: strong agents beat one another in
circles~\cite{angliss2026vgcbench}. The target is therefore not a high score but a
strategy nobody can punish, an unexploitable equilibrium, which is exactly what
the equilibrium machinery of the previous subsection is meant to provide.

\paragraph{Team building inverts the risk.}
Adding team building inverts the risk, from too trivial to too big. Team building
is an outer game whose payoff for a team is itself a battle equilibrium, so the two
phases form a bilevel game over a structured combinatorial space (teams have
synergy and type coverage, not opaque policies), which points to population methods
such as PSRO, double oracle, and EGTA~\cite{lanctot2017psro}. This is where the
deepest novelty of the problem lives. The same move is also where scope risk lies:
the full coupled problem at a superhuman target is plausibly too large for one PhD,
a tension the scoping in Section~\ref{subsec:scoping} must resolve.

\subsection{Redefining and Measuring ``Superhuman'' Under Variance}
\label{subsec:superhuman}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
% DONE (D) "Superhuman" must be REDEFINED: genuine RNG means a perfect Nash agent
%     still loses winnable games -> cannot mean ~100% win rate. Reframe as best
%     achievable EXPECTED win rate / LOWEST exploitability given variance. Give a
%     working definition. [src: vgc_comparison Q2, Q3]
% DONE (E) Make variance-aware EVALUATION a first-class contribution (publishable on
%     its own): separating decision quality from luck. Anchors:
%     - sample-size reality: poker needs ~30k hands to separate pro from amateur.
%     - binomial-proportion confidence intervals on win rate.
%     - variance-reduction techniques (e.g. AIVAT) for imperfect-info agent eval.
%     [src: vgc_comparison Q3,Q4; websearch AIVAT, importance-sampling eval]

Genuine randomness means that even a perfect equilibrium agent loses games it
should win, so ``superhuman'' cannot mean a near-perfect win rate. The workable
definition is the best achievable expected win rate, or equivalently the lowest
exploitability, given the variance the game imposes. This redefinition forces an
evaluation problem, which we treat as a first-class contribution: separating
decision quality from luck is hard and publishable in its own right. The scale of
the problem is well documented in poker, where a heads-up man-machine match of
80{,}000 hands played over thirteen days was still only on the edge of statistical
significance~\cite{burch2018aivat}. Three tools carry over to VGC: reporting
binomial-proportion confidence intervals on win rate rather than point estimates;
using enough games for those intervals to separate agents; and applying
variance-reduction techniques such as AIVAT, which was built precisely to evaluate
agents in imperfect-information games and cut the games needed for a given
conclusion by more than an order of magnitude~\cite{burch2018aivat}.

\subsection{Scoping a Finishable PhD}
\label{subsec:scoping}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
% DONE (G) THE key guardrail: decide early which loop is the RESEARCH and which is
%     "good-enough" FROZEN infrastructure (team search vs. battle solver). Do not
%     let the inner battle solver eat year three. [src: vgc_comparison Q4 closing]
\paragraph{Scoping team building, not battling.}
Battling is firmly in scope: it is the inner loop, built to be strong and then
relied on. The scoping decision lives entirely in the outer loop, because team
building in full is too large, both the astronomical configuration space and the
bilevel coupling shown in Section~\ref{subsec:triviality}. Feasibility therefore
comes from narrowing team building rather than abandoning it. A few illustrative
handles: restrict to a single limited-regulation metagame, work over a curated
pool of teams rather than open construction, or fix the team representation so the
outer search ranges over a small structured set. The point is not to pick one of
these now but to commit early to some bound on the outer loop, so that the coupled
problem stays finishable.
